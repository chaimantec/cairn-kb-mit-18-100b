# 18.100B — Real Analysis (MIT, Spring 2025)

MIT's introduction to analysis, taught by **Tobias Holck Colding** and published on [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-100b-real-analysis-spring-2025/). The course has two goals: learning to write mathematical proofs, and proving the theorems of calculus rigorously. According to the syllabus it covers real numbers, limits, convergence, series and continuity, then metric spaces, differentiation and Riemann integrals, and ends with ordinary differential equations (existence and uniqueness via Picard–Lindelöf). The main textbook is Thomson, Bruckner and Bruckner, *Elementary Real Analysis*, 2nd ed. (TBB), which is free online; Rudin's *Principles of Mathematical Analysis* is the second text. Colding lectures at the blackboard and types lecture notes for every lecture. There are no slides.

**Coverage: Lectures 1 to 7 only**, the first seven of 25 videos (23 lectures and two exam reviews). For any other lecture this knowledge base has nothing yet. Say so rather than answering from general knowledge, because a learner will take an answer here as what this course teaches. The [lecture list](#all-lectures) below gives each video's status.

## How to read this knowledge base

- Start from the **wiki** pages below. They cite the transcript as (≈M:SS) and the lecture notes as "notes p. N".
- **Transcripts:** read the edited ones in [`raw/transcripts/`](raw/transcripts/). They are a light copy-edit of OCW's professional captions, with every uncertain spot marked *[Ed: …]*. Student speech is marked only where the captions label it ("STUDENT:" in Lectures 1 to 6, "AUDIENCE:" in Lecture 7). The verbatim captions are in [`raw/transcripts/original/`](raw/transcripts/original/).
- **Lecture notes:** Colding's typed notes for each lecture, transcribed page by page with the mathematics in LaTeX, are in [`raw/notes/`](raw/notes/). Each notes file lists its printed typos under "Printed slips".
- **Notation:** the notes set the number systems in bold, $\mathbf{N}$, $\mathbf{Z}$, $\mathbf{Q}$, $\mathbf{R}$, and a field as $\mathbf{F}$, and this knowledge base follows them. In this course $\mathbf{N} = \lbrace 1, 2, 3, \cdots \rbrace$ does not contain $0$.
- **Where the board and the notes differ** (the form of a law, how much detail a proof shows), the wiki follows the notes and gives the board's version with its timestamp.
- **Images:** this knowledge base has none, and the notes for Lectures 1 to 7 contain no figures. Never construct an image path.

## Contents

### Lecture pages

- [Lecture 1 — Introduction to Real Numbers](wiki/01-introduction-to-real-numbers.md). Course logistics and grading; the intermediate value theorem as motivation; Q1–Q3 about $\sqrt{2}$ and the answer that $\mathbf{R}$ is a complete ordered field containing $\mathbf{Q}$; $\mathbf{N}$, $\mathbf{Z}$, $\mathbf{Q}$; equality, addition, multiplication and order on $\mathbf{Q}$; the proof that multiplication is well defined; the eleven field axioms; why $\mathbf{N}$ and $\mathbf{Z}$ are not fields; ordered sets and ordered fields; proofs that the zero is unique and that $x \lt y$, $z \gt 0$ imply $x z \lt y z$. Completeness is deferred.
- [Lecture 2 — Introduction to Real Numbers (cont.)](wiki/02-introduction-to-real-numbers-cont.md). The proof that no rational number squares to $2$; $\sqrt{2}$ as a limit of decimals (a preview); upper bounds, least upper bounds and completeness (the least upper bound property); the existence of $\mathbf{R}$, taken for granted; the proof that $\sqrt{2} \in \mathbf{R}$ in two steps; $\mathbf{Q}$ is not complete; the Archimedean property; a rational number between any two reals; the closing student questions; and a list of where the board and the notes differ.
- [Lecture 3 — How to Write a Proof; Archimedean Property](wiki/03-how-to-write-a-proof-archimedean-property.md). Course materials (the notes, TBB versus Rudin); upper and lower bounds, $\sup A$ and $\inf A$, and why a bound depends on the surrounding ordered set; scrap paper versus a formal proof; the formal proof that some $\alpha \gt 0$ has $\alpha^2 = 2$, including the board detour Colding uses as a lesson; which properties of $\mathbf{R}$ it uses; the formal proof that $\mathbf{N}$ is not bounded from above; the definition of a sequence. From the notes only: the corollary that $\frac{1}{n} \lt \epsilon$ for some $n$, and the definition of convergence.
- [Lecture 4 — Sequences; Convergence](wiki/04-sequences-convergence.md). Sequences and the examples $(-1)^n$ and $\frac{1}{n}$; the definition of convergence with $\epsilon$ and $N$, its picture, and divergence; the proof that $0.999 \cdots = 1$ from the Archimedean property; a convergent sequence is bounded; the four algebraic properties of limits (constant multiples, sums, products, quotients), with the board's full proof for products and the halfway trick; subsequences by example and by definition, and the theorem that $a_n \to a$ exactly when every subsequence converges to $a$; the students' questions; and where the board and the notes differ.
- [Lecture 5 — Monotone Convergence Theorem](wiki/05-monotone-convergence-theorem.md). A review of sequences, subsequences, convergence and the algebraic rules (stated with $\frac{1}{a_n}$); the worked example $\frac{n^2 + 1}{n^2 + n + 1} \to 1$; monotone increasing and decreasing sequences; the monotone convergence theorem (a bounded monotone sequence converges to the $\sup$ or $\inf$ of its terms) and its proof; the board-only construction of two monotone sequences from any bounded sequence (the $\sup$ and $\inf$ of its tails), which Colding says is how limsup and liminf are defined; $\sqrt{2}$ as the limit of its decimals, $a_n = \frac{b_n}{10^{n-1}}$, proved increasing, bounded and with $a^2 = 2$; the definition of a Cauchy sequence and the theorem, stated without proof, that a sequence converges if and only if it is Cauchy; contracting maps, fixed points and the contracting mapping theorem, stated without proof; Cauchy's biography (notes only); and where the board and the notes differ.
- [Lecture 6 — Cauchy Convergence Theorem](wiki/06-cauchy-convergence-theorem.md). A review of the monotone convergence theorem and Cauchy sequences; the theorem that a Cauchy sequence is bounded; the Cauchy convergence theorem, with Colding's claim that the converse was proved last time (no such proof is in this knowledge base); contracting maps and fixed points, the student's question about the constant $c$, and the lemma that there is at most one fixed point; the Problem Set 3 exercise that iterating a contracting map gives a Cauchy sequence, and the board's estimate for consecutive terms; the proof of the contracting mapping theorem from the Cauchy convergence theorem; Newton's method, $T(x) = x - \frac{f(x)}{f'(x)}$, as a contracting map near a good guess; the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem (stated, proof promised for next time) and the proof that every Cauchy sequence converges; the definition of continuity with $\epsilon$ and $\delta$ and the theorem that a continuous function takes convergent sequences to convergent sequences; the extreme value theorem on $[a, b]$ and its proof, with a student's question about it; and where the board and the notes differ.
- [Lecture 7 — Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem; Cauchy Sequences; Series](wiki/07-bolzano-weierstrass-theorem-cauchy-sequences-series.md). The proof of the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem (every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence) by halving intervals, with the subsequence squeezed between the increasing left endpoints $b_k$ and the decreasing right endpoints $c_k$, $c_k - b_k = 2^{1-k}$, and the students' questions about it; Weierstrass's biography (notes only); series as sequences of partial sums, the notation $\sum a_i$ for both the series and its limit, and why the starting index does not affect convergence; Achilles and the turtle; the geometric series, $(1 - c) \sum_{i=0}^{n} c^i = 1 - c^{n+1}$, its sum $\frac{1}{1-c}$ for $\vert c \vert \lt 1$ and its divergence otherwise; the divergence of the harmonic series by induction, $s_{2^n - 1} \geq \frac{n}{2}$; the check that the terms go to $0$; series of non-negative terms; absolute convergence, the alternating harmonic series, and the proof that absolute convergence implies convergence; the convergence of $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ by comparison with a geometric series; the list of convergence tests and the first comparison test; a student's question and the idea of the integral test; from the notes only, a second comparison test and the ratio test; and where the board and the notes differ.

### Topic pages

- [The real numbers](wiki/real-numbers.md). The course's one-sentence characterization of $\mathbf{R}$ as a complete ordered field containing $\mathbf{Q}$, why it starts there, and where each part is explained (Lectures 1 to 7).
- [The rational numbers](wiki/rational-numbers.md). $\mathbf{Q}$ as quotients $\frac{m}{n}$ with $m \in \mathbf{Z}$, $n \in \mathbf{N}$; when two quotients are equal; the operations and the order; why well-definedness must be checked; $\mathbf{Q}$ as an ordered field that is not complete; a rational number between any two reals.
- [Fields and ordered fields](wiki/fields-and-ordered-fields.md). The eleven field axioms in a table, ordered sets, the two ordered-field axioms, the theorems proved from them (and the uniqueness of $1$), the examples and non-examples ($\mathbf{Q}$; $\mathbf{N}$, $\mathbf{Z}$), and what makes an ordered field complete.
- [The intermediate value theorem](wiki/intermediate-value-theorem.md). The statement as given in Lecture 1, the chalk-on-the-board intuition for continuity, and why the picture is not a proof. Lecture 6 defines continuity rigorously but does not prove the theorem; the course proves it in Lecture 11, which is not yet covered.
- [Completeness and the least upper bound property](wiki/least-upper-bound-property.md). Upper bound, least upper bound, complete ordered set and field; the theorem that a complete ordered field containing $\mathbf{Q}$ exists (not proved in the course); what completeness proves in Lectures 2 and 3, and its role in Lecture 5's proof of the monotone convergence theorem.
- [Supremum and infimum](wiki/supremum-and-infimum.md). Upper and lower bounds, "bounded", $\sup A$ and $\inf A$ with their defining properties; why a bound need not lie in the set and why the surrounding ordered set matters (Lecture 3's half-line example); where $\sup$ and $\inf$ are used so far, including the bound on a convergent sequence, the limit of a bounded monotone sequence, the $\sup$ and $\inf$ of the tails of a bounded sequence (Lecture 5), and the $\sup$ and $\inf$ of a continuous function on $[a, b]$, which are attained (Lecture 6); limsup and liminf (Lecture 9) are not yet covered.
- [The square root of 2](wiki/square-root-of-2.md). Lecture 1's questions about $\sqrt{2}$ and Lecture 2's answers: the definition, the proof that it is not rational, the proof that it is real (as a least upper bound), and the decimals that approach it; Lecture 3's formal version of the proof; and Lecture 5's proof, by the monotone convergence theorem, that the decimals converge to a number whose square is $2$.
- [The Archimedean property](wiki/archimedean-property.md). Every real number is less than some natural number: the statement, its proof from completeness, and the corollary that a rational number lies between any two reals; Lecture 3's formal proof that $\mathbf{N}$ is not bounded from above, the notes' corollary that $\frac{1}{n} \lt \epsilon$ for some $n$, its use in Lecture 4's proof that $0.999 \cdots = 1$, and Lecture 5's appeal to it for $\frac{1}{n} \to 0$.
- [Writing proofs](wiki/writing-proofs.md). Colding's case for rigor, the level of detail he expects, Lecture 1's three model proofs, Lecture 2's remarks on proof by contradiction and on proving an equality as two inequalities, Lecture 3's structure for a formal proof (scrap paper first; say what you will show and where the contradiction comes from; define $h$ up front), Lecture 4's habits for proofs with $\epsilon$ and $N$ (apply the hypothesis to the number you need, choose $N$ last, compare through something halfway between), Lecture 5's advice to "analyze what you have to prove" before writing it down nicely, Lecture 6's proofs from a theorem promised for later and from a homework exercise, Lecture 7's proofs by induction and Colding's remark that "you don't start with the claim", and how the board and the notes differ.
- [Sequences and limits](wiki/sequences-and-limits.md). A sequence as a function from $\mathbf{N}$ to $\mathbf{R}$; the examples (the decimals of $\sqrt{2}$, $(-1)^n$, $\frac{1}{n}$); the definition of convergence with $\epsilon$ and $N$, how to read it, and divergence; $0.999 \cdots = 1$; a convergent sequence is bounded; monotone sequences and the monotone convergence theorem (Lecture 5); Cauchy sequences, and Lecture 6's proof that every Cauchy sequence converges; series as sequences of partial sums (Lecture 7).
- [Algebraic properties of limits](wiki/algebraic-properties-of-limits.md). If $a_n \to a$ and $b_n \to b$, then $C a_n \to C a$, $a_n + b_n \to a + b$, $a_n b_n \to a b$ and $\frac{a_n}{b_n} \to \frac{a}{b}$ (when $b_n, b \neq 0$): the statement as in the Lecture 4 notes, a proof of each, how each proof chooses its $\epsilon$, and the trick of comparing through a term halfway between; and Lecture 5's review, its worked example $\frac{n^2 + 1}{n^2 + n + 1} \to 1$, and the rules' use in the proof about $\sqrt{2}$; and their use for the sum of the geometric series (Lecture 7).
- [Subsequences](wiki/subsequences.md). Examples and non-examples (repeated terms, terms out of order), the definition as $f \circ g$ with $g : \mathbf{N} \to \mathbf{N}$ strictly increasing, the notation $a_{n_k}$, and the theorem that a sequence converges to $a$ exactly when all its subsequences do (Lecture 4). Lecture 5 reviews the definition and reads $n_k$ as $g(k)$. Lecture 6 states the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem and uses convergent subsequences in two proofs; Lecture 7 constructs such a subsequence and uses the subsequences $s_{2^n - 1}$ of partial sums in two proofs about series.
- [The monotone convergence theorem](wiki/monotone-convergence-theorem.md). Monotone increasing and decreasing sequences; the theorem that a monotone sequence bounded in the right direction converges to the $\sup$ or $\inf$ of its terms, and its proof from the least upper bound; the $\sup$ and $\inf$ of the tails of any bounded sequence as two monotone sequences; the application to $\sqrt{2}$ as the limit of its decimals; the facts Lecture 5 uses without proof; and its uses in Lecture 7, for the endpoints in the Bolzano–Weierstrass proof and for series of non-negative terms.
- [Cauchy sequences](wiki/cauchy-sequences.md). The definition ($\vert a_n - a_m \vert \lt \epsilon$ for $m, n \geq N$) and how it differs from convergence (Lecture 5); the theorem that a Cauchy sequence is bounded, and the proof that every Cauchy sequence converges, from the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem (Lecture 6, with the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem proved in Lecture 7); its use for absolute convergence (Lecture 7); the converse, that a convergent sequence is Cauchy, is stated and used in Lecture 7 but not proved in this knowledge base.
- [The contracting mapping theorem](wiki/contracting-mapping-theorem.md). Fixed points, contracting maps $T : \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ with a constant $c \lt 1$, and the theorem that a contracting map has exactly one fixed point (stated in Lecture 5, proved in Lecture 6 from the Cauchy convergence theorem and a Problem Set 3 exercise); the iteration $a_{n+1} = T(a_n)$; Newton's method as an application; and why it matters: in more general spaces it gives solutions to differential equations.
- [The Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem](wiki/bolzano-weierstrass-theorem.md). Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence: the statement; Lecture 7's proof by halving intervals and squeezing a subsequence between two monotone sequences, with the notes' version; and its two uses in Lecture 6, where it was assumed, for the Cauchy convergence theorem and the extreme value theorem.
- [Continuous functions](wiki/continuous-functions.md). Lecture 1's informal picture, Lecture 6's definition of continuity at a point with $\epsilon$ and $\delta$ and the picture behind it, and the theorem, with proof, that a continuous function takes convergent sequences to convergent sequences.
- [The extreme value theorem](wiki/extreme-value-theorem.md). A continuous function on $[a, b]$ attains its $\sup$ and $\inf$ (Lecture 6): the statement, the proof from the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, why the endpoints matter, the student's question about the sequence, and what the proof leaves to the reader.
- [Series](wiki/series.md). A series as the sequence $s_n$ of partial sums of $a_n$, convergence, the notation $\sum a_i$ for the series and for its limit, why the starting index does not affect convergence, the check that $a_n \to 0$ (a series whose terms do not tend to $0$ diverges), series of non-negative terms (convergent exactly when the partial sums are bounded), and absolute convergence with the proof that it implies convergence (Lecture 7).
- [The geometric series](wiki/geometric-series.md). $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} c^i$: the formula $\frac{1 - c^{n+1}}{1 - c}$ for the partial sums, convergence to $\frac{1}{1-c}$ when $\vert c \vert \lt 1$, the board's and the notes' proofs of divergence when $\vert c \vert \geq 1$, and its role as the series others are compared with (Lecture 7).
- [The harmonic series](wiki/harmonic-series.md). The divergence of $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ by induction ($s_{2^n - 1} \geq \frac{n}{2}$); the alternating harmonic series $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$, convergent but not absolutely (its convergence is promised for later); and the convergence of $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ by comparing partial sums with a geometric series (Lecture 7).
- [Convergence tests for series](wiki/convergence-tests-for-series.md). The tests Lecture 7 names (comparison, ratio, root, integral, alternating series); the comparison test with $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ and its example; from the notes, the second comparison test (limit of $\frac{a_n}{b_n}$) and the ratio test with its two inconclusive examples; the integral test as sketched. None is proved yet.
